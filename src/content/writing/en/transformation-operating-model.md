## A roadmap describes work. It does not govern change

In a complex transformation, new information appears faster than a central plan can be updated. That does not make the roadmap useless. It means the roadmap is insufficient on its own.

BCG’s 2024 analysis found that only 26% of corporate transformations created value in both the short and long term. That is not the same as saying every other transformation failed. It does show how difficult it is to sustain value across the full transformation horizon.

The usual response is to improve the roadmap: more milestones, more dependencies and more reporting. That can improve visibility, but it does not answer the harder questions. Who can change priority? Which evidence can reopen a decision? Which choices belong with delivery teams, and which need a broader mandate?

That is why transformation needs an operating model, not only a plan.

## Five elements make the model operational

I find five elements particularly useful.

**Outcome.** What change is the transformation trying to create, and how will we know it matters?

**Decision rights.** Who can change scope, sequence, resources or accountability?

**Cadence.** Which decisions happen in which forum, and how often does evidence reach them?

**Evidence.** Which measures test whether assumptions, benefits and risks remain valid?

**Revisit rules.** What condition allows or forces an earlier decision to be reopened?

These elements turn a roadmap from a fixed sequence into a governed system of choices. They also prevent a common failure mode: adding roles and committees before defining the work those roles are meant to resolve.

## An operating model is more than boxes and lines

McKinsey’s 2025 cross-industry survey of 2,000 executives found that 63% said their operating-model redesigns met most objectives and improved performance, compared with 21% in a comparable 2014 survey. The share of redesigns reported as completed and implemented also rose from 51% to 79%.

Those are survey results, not an objective census of all redesigns. The useful point is what sits behind them: operating-model work increasingly extends beyond organization charts toward workflows, decision rights and the way work actually moves across the organization.

A new steering committee, PMO structure or set of job descriptions may change the organization chart without changing the operating model. The real test is whether decisions move differently.

## Governance should remove conflict, not produce meetings

Every recurring forum should have a defined decision type, mandate and input. If the same issue circulates between steering committees, architecture, PMO and product teams without a clear owner, the problem is not a lack of meetings. It is the architecture of decisions.

A mature operating model reduces the number of places where a decision can get stuck and improves the information available at the moment of choice. It also preserves autonomy where a local team can responsibly own the consequence.

MIT CISR’s work on operating models and enterprise architecture has long emphasized the link between the way a business operates and the foundation needed for execution. I would add one explicit management question: who is allowed to change the plan when execution produces new evidence?

## Follow one decision through the system

Imagine three initiatives depending on the same platform team. The roadmap shows three delivery dates. Execution shows that the shared dependency is now the real constraint.

A good operating model must resolve the conflict. The portfolio identifies which outcome now deserves priority. The relevant owner adjusts resources. Architecture checks whether the new sequence creates a longer-term dependency. Delivery teams adapt their local plans without escalating every reversible choice. The evidence and rationale are recorded so the choice can be reviewed instead of rediscovered in the next meeting.

The transformation has not lost control because the roadmap changed. It has demonstrated control because the organization knows why it changed, who owned the trade-off and what evidence should reopen the decision.

## Adaptability and accountability are not opposites

Poorly designed governance treats control as preserving the original plan. That creates a bad incentive: teams learn more, but the organization becomes less willing to change because change looks like failure.

A better definition of control is traceability. The organization can explain which assumption changed, which evidence mattered, who owned the choice and what consequence was accepted.

That creates a healthier role for a transformation office or PMO: not to defend the baseline at all costs, but to make the decision system visible and keep evidence flowing into it.

## Test one transformation forum

Choose one recurring steering or governance meeting. For each standing agenda item ask:

1. What decision belongs here?
2. Who owns it?
3. What evidence must be present?
4. What can be decided elsewhere without escalation?
5. What condition reopens the decision later?

If most agenda items are status updates, the forum is primarily a reporting mechanism. If decisions have owners, evidence and boundaries, it is part of the operating model.

A roadmap tells the organization what it expected to happen. An operating model tells it how to remain coherent when reality disagrees.
