## A roadmap is a hypothesis about execution

In a complex transformation, new information appears faster than a central plan can be updated. That does not make the roadmap useless. It means the roadmap represents the organization's best current knowledge, not a contract with the future.

BCG reported in 2024 that only 26% of corporate transformations created value in both the short and long term. That does not mean the remaining 74% were complete failures. It shows how difficult it is to sustain value across the full transformation horizon.

A common response to a gap between plan and reality is to add more detail to the roadmap: more milestones, dependencies and reporting. That increases visibility but does not tell the organization **when new information is important enough to change an earlier decision**.

Transformation therefore needs more than a plan. It needs an operating model that can adapt the plan without losing accountability.

## Adaptation needs rules, not permanent reopening

New evidence should not automatically trigger a new debate about every earlier choice. That would quickly turn adaptability into decision churn.

A mature operating model separates three things.

**Evidence.** What has genuinely changed our understanding of value, risk, dependency or execution feasibility?

**Trigger.** Which condition justifies reopening the decision?

**Mandate.** Who can change direction, scope, sequence or resource allocation?

Cadence matters as well. Some decisions should react quickly; others should move only through defined review cycles. Adaptability does not mean continuously renegotiating the plan. It means the organization knows **when it is allowed to change it**.

Direction describes the outcome that justifies change. The assumption explains why a particular initiative, sequence or dependency should produce that outcome. Execution generates evidence. Only then does the operating model decide whether to preserve the choice or reopen it.

In McKinsey's 2025 survey of 2,000 executives across industries, 63% said their operating model redesign met most objectives and improved organizational performance. In a comparable 2014 survey, the figure was 21%. The share of redesigns reported as completed and implemented rose from 51% to 79%.

These are survey results rather than an objective registry of redesigns. The more interesting point is what sits behind the shift: operating models increasingly describe not only structure but also workflow, decision rights and how organizational units work together.

A new steering committee, PMO structure or set of role descriptions can change the organization on paper without changing its adaptation mechanism. The real test is whether new evidence can reach a place where it can actually change a decision.

## One dependency can change the whole plan

Imagine three initiatives that depend on the same platform team. The roadmap shows three dates. Execution reveals that the shared dependency has become the real bottleneck.

A good operating model does not automatically answer “escalate”. It first identifies which trigger has been met and who has the mandate to change the earlier decision.

Portfolio governance may decide which outcome now has priority. The appropriate owner may move resources. Architecture may test whether the new sequence creates an expensive long-term dependency. Delivery teams can adjust local plans without reopening every reversible decision.

Transformation does not lose control because the plan changes. It loses control when nobody can explain **why** it changed, **who** made the decision and **which evidence** mattered.

## Traceability is a better definition of control than baseline compliance

Poorly designed governance equates control with preserving the original plan. That creates a bad incentive: teams learn more while the organization becomes less willing to change direction because every change looks like failure.

A better definition of control is traceability. The organization can show which assumption changed, which evidence triggered reconsideration, who had the mandate to decide and which consequence was accepted.

This also creates a healthier role for a transformation office or PMO: not defending the baseline at any cost, but maintaining visibility of assumptions, evidence and decisions — while preventing triggers from becoming either dead rules or excuses for constantly resetting the plan.

## Trace one decision from assumption to change

Instead of evaluating the entire transformation forum, pick one important decision and answer five things.

**Assumption.** What had to be true for the decision to make sense?

**Owner.** Who had the mandate to make it?

**Evidence.** What do we now know differently?

**Trigger.** Which condition allows the decision to be reopened?

**Last decision.** What exactly changed — or was consciously left unchanged?

If that chain cannot be traced, the roadmap may describe the work, but the operating model is not managing adaptation.

A roadmap says what was supposed to happen. An operating model says how to preserve accountability when reality provides better information.
