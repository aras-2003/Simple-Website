# Design Principles — current visual direction

## North star

arkadiuszkamrowski.com should feel like an executive strategy publication transformed into an interactive digital system.

It is not:
- a résumé,
- a portfolio template,
- a consulting landing page,
- a SaaS website.

The visitor should feel that the site demonstrates systems thinking before the text explicitly says so.

## Desired reaction

The intended reaction is not "this website has lots of animations".

It is:
1. "this is unusually well designed";
2. "the visual system reinforces what he is talking about";
3. "this feels credible for a senior executive audience".

## Visual formula

Executive editorial design + systems thinking + spatial interaction + motion + technological precision.

## Complexity model

Keep the visual vocabulary simple.

Prefer:
- lines,
- planes,
- grids,
- nodes,
- typography,
- light,
- layers,
- simple solids,
- structured diagrams.

Create richness through:
- motion,
- relationships,
- depth,
- state changes,
- intersection,
- transparency,
- scale,
- timing.

Complexity should emerge from behavior, not from complicated shapes.

## Motion philosophy

Motion is a first-class part of the design system.

Use it to express verbs such as:
- connect,
- separate,
- converge,
- prioritize,
- expand,
- collapse,
- align,
- flow,
- transform,
- resolve,
- focus.

These verbs are more useful than generic instructions such as "animate this section".

## Hero

The Hero should immediately establish visual identity.

It should include a persistent or evolving visual system rather than only a one-time entrance animation.

The page should feel alive within the first seconds, without competing with the primary positioning statement.

## Scroll narrative

Scrolling should create progression.

Sections may:
- transform previous structures,
- reuse visual elements,
- transition between states,
- reveal relationships,
- evolve the background context.

Whenever possible, avoid making every section feel like an unrelated independent card.

## Background

The background is an active part of the visual system.

It may evolve across sections through:
- slow spatial motion,
- structured light,
- gradients used with intent,
- geometric fields,
- contextual animation,
- depth changes.

Background motion must never reduce readability.

## Depth

Use depth as a major differentiator where it strengthens the story.

Possible techniques:
- layered planes,
- controlled perspective,
- overlapping typography,
- parallax,
- translucent surfaces,
- light and shadow,
- Z-axis transformations.

Depth should feel architectural rather than decorative.

## Professionalism

The experience is designed for senior professional audiences.

It may be visually ambitious.

It should not feel:
- playful,
- juvenile,
- trendy for the sake of trend,
- intentionally strange,
- difficult to understand.

Sophistication should come from execution quality.

## Performance boundary

Visual ambition is allowed to consume reasonable resources.

Do not flatten the experience merely to maximize a synthetic score.

However, do not accept:
- visible jank,
- blocked interaction,
- persistently high idle CPU/GPU use,
- disproportionate payload,
- animations that make common laptops or phones feel slow.

Prefer native techniques first. Introduce a motion or graphics dependency only when the experience improvement is material and implementation without it would be disproportionately brittle or complex.

## Mobile

Mobile is not a static fallback.

Preserve the same visual idea with adapted:
- scale,
- layer count,
- motion amplitude,
- interaction model,
- timing.

Pointer-specific effects must have equivalent non-pointer behavior.

## Reference quality

When evaluating a concept, ask whether it would look credible on:
- a premium technology company,
- a high-end strategy publication,
- an architecture or design studio,
- an executive-level digital experience.

If it resembles a standard website template, the visual concept is not strong enough.
